\begin{abstract}
Recognizing textual entailment (RTE) asks whether the meaning of a hypothesis can be inferred from a premise. \citet{khader2016textual} addressed this problem for Arabic with stemmed word overlap, synonym expansion, and bigram thresholds, reporting 61\% overall precision and recall on a 100-pair sample of ArbTEDS. We keep their scientific question---entailment outside English, in a morphologically rich language---but change the language, data, algorithm, and training method. Specifically, we fine-tune XLM-RoBERTa-base on the Hindi split of XNLI for three-way natural language inference (entailment, neutral, contradiction). Evaluation uses the standard 5{,}010-pair XNLI-Hindi test set rather than a 100-pair subsample. On Apple Silicon we document a training-stack failure (fused AdamW and SDPA) that collapsed the classifier to chance (33.3\% accuracy, loss pinned at $\ln 3$), and we replace it with eager attention and unfused AdamW. \TBD{After the full run, replace with: the model reaches XX.X\% test accuracy and YY.Y\% macro-F1.} The assignment required at least two modifications of an existing paper; this work implements five (language, dataset, algorithm, methodology, and a full code release).

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Textual entailment, also called natural language inference (NLI), is the problem of deciding whether a hypothesis $H$ is true given a text or premise $T$~\parencite{dagan2006rte,bowman2015snli}. A system that cannot tell whether one sentence licenses another cannot reliably summarize, answer questions, or filter retrieved passages: those applications all reduce, at some step, to ``does this sentence follow from that one?''

The PASCAL Recognising Textual Entailment challenges~\parencite{dagan2006rte} framed the modern English evaluation as a two-way decision (\emph{entails} / \emph{does not entail}). Later English corpora, especially SNLI~\parencite{bowman2015snli} and MultiNLI~\parencite{williams2018snli}, added a third \emph{neutral} label for cases where $T$ neither supports nor contradicts $H$. Most published neural NLI work still concentrates on English. Work on Arabic, Hindi, and other morphologically rich languages remains thinner, even though those languages are exactly where surface-form matching is least reliable.

\subsection{The paper we adapt}
\citet{khader2016textual} studied RTE for Arabic. Arabic has optional diacritics (so a single undiacritized token can have several readings), flexible word order (VSO, SVO, and others), and a large synonym inventory. Their system is a pipeline of preprocessing (named-entity marks, POS tags from MSTParser and MADA, ISRI stemming), stop-word removal, unigram overlap with synonym credit, and hypothesis-normalized bigram overlap. Each overlap score is compared to a threshold chosen on a development sample. The decision is binary: Entails or NotEntails. On 100 randomly selected pairs from ArbTEDS~\parencite{alabbas2013dataset} they report 68\% precision / 64\% recall for Entails, 58\% / 60\% for NotEntails, and 61\% overall precision and recall (their Table~6; 61 of 100 pairs judged correctly in their Table~5).

That design has four structural limits. First, thresholds and bag-of-word overlap do not model long-range context or compositional negation. Second, the authors themselves note, following \citet{alkhawaldeh2015negation}, that treating negation as a stop word can reverse polarity. Third, evaluation is a 100-pair subsample of a 618-pair corpus, so confidence intervals are wide. Fourth, the feature stack (ISRI stemmer, Arabic WordNet-style synonyms, CoNLL trees) does not transfer to Hindi.

\subsection{What we change}
We keep the same research question---can we recognize entailment in a morphologically rich language other than English---and change the implementation along the axes the course assignment allows (Table~\ref{tab:options}). Hindi, like Arabic, has rich morphology and relatively free word order, and it is written in Devanagari rather than Arabic script, so a successful transfer cannot be a copy-paste of stemmers and lexicons. We therefore replace the lexical pipeline with a pretrained multilingual Transformer~\parencite{conneau2020xlmr} fine-tuned on XNLI Hindi~\parencite{conneau2018xnli}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{English RTE and NLI}
Early RTE systems mixed WordNet, logical inference, n-gram overlap, and syntactic matching~\parencite{dagan2006rte}. \citet{du2010recognize} combine unigram overlap with WordNet and give extra weight to POS and named entities---a recipe close to \citet{khader2016textual}. \citet{pakray2011textual} add an SVM over 23 lexical features plus a rule-based syntactic tag, scoring the mid-50s on RTE-4. These systems show that overlap is a surprisingly strong baseline when $T$ and $H$ share many content words, and a weak one when entailment depends on a single negation, a paraphrase, or a world-knowledge fact.

Large annotated corpora changed the method. SNLI~\parencite{bowman2015snli} provided hundreds of thousands of English pairs; MultiNLI~\parencite{williams2018snli} added genre diversity. Neural classifiers trained on those corpora quickly overtook feature pipelines. BERT~\parencite{devlin2019bert} made the standard recipe: concatenate $T$ and $H$, encode with a Transformer~\parencite{vaswani2017attention}, and classify the first-token state. RoBERTa~\parencite{liu2019roberta} kept the architecture and improved the pretraining recipe (more data, no next-sentence prediction, dynamic masking).

\subsection{Arabic textual entailment}
Arabic TE is small as a literature. \citet{alabbas2011arbte} first laid out why off-the-shelf English RTE tools fail on Arabic (diacritics, flexible order, parser noise) and experimented with tree-edit distance on dependency trees. \citet{alabbas2013jair} extend TED with subtree operations. ArbTEDS~\parencite{alabbas2013dataset} collects 618 news headline--article pairs, triple-annotated, covering sport, politics, business, and general news. \citet{alkhawaldeh2015negation} show that dropping Arabic negation as a stop word flips Entails/NotEntails. \citet{khader2016textual} sit in this line: they keep the ArbTEDS setting and a lexical/semantic pipeline rather than TED, and they evaluate with RTE-2 style precision and recall.

\subsection{Cross-lingual NLI and XLM-R}
XNLI~\parencite{conneau2018xnli} translates MultiNLI development and test data into 14 other languages (15 including English), among them Hindi, and provides a training corpus in each language. Professional translators produce the evaluation sets, so the Hindi test set is not crowd-sourced paraphrase of Hindi news; it is translated English NLI. That is a limitation we return to in Section~\ref{sec:disc}.

Cross-lingual language model pretraining~\parencite{lample2019xlm} showed that a shared subword vocabulary and a masked-language-model objective transfer to XNLI. XLM-RoBERTa~\parencite{conneau2020xlmr} scales that idea: RoBERTa-style training on 2.5\,TB of CommonCrawl in 100 languages, including Hindi, with a 250k SentencePiece vocabulary~\parencite{kudo2018sentencepiece}. The base model we use has 12 layers, hidden size 768, and about 278M parameters once a three-way classification head is attached. \citet{conneau2020xlmr} report XLM-R-base in the mid-70s accuracy on XNLI Hindi; XLM-R-large is higher at much larger cost. We implement fine-tuning with Hugging Face Transformers~\parencite{wolf2020transformers}.

\section{Why Hindi}
\label{sec:hindi}

The assignment asks for a \emph{different language}, not a random one. Hindi is a reasonable counterpart to Arabic for RTE for four reasons.

\paragraph{Morphology.}
Hindi is Indo-Aryan, not Semitic, but it is still morphologically rich: verbs inflect for tense, aspect, mood, and honorifics; nouns inflect for number, case, and gender. A surface unigram match therefore misses inflected forms of the same lemma in the same way Arabic overlap misses undiacritized roots. Subword SentencePiece units~\parencite{kudo2018sentencepiece} are a coarse substitute for stemming: they split frequent suffixes rather than recovering a linguistic root, but they do not require an ISRI-style analyser.

\paragraph{Word order.}
Hindi is canonically SOV and allows scrambling of arguments. Entailment can survive a permutation of the same case-marked noun phrases, which bag-of-word overlap captures and which bigrams can miss. A Transformer attends across the whole 128-token window, so order is a feature rather than a hard template.

\paragraph{Script and tokenization.}
Devanagari is an abugida, not an alphabet. Whitespace tokenization already yields akshara sequences; XLM-R's 250k vocabulary includes Hindi pieces learned from CommonCrawl~\parencite{conneau2020xlmr}. We do not transliterate, unlike ArbTEDS's Buckwalter form~\parencite{alabbas2013dataset}.

\paragraph{Resource profile.}
Hindi has web-scale unlabeled text (hence XLM-R) and a public NLI benchmark (XNLI), but it does not have a widely used 618-pair news TE corpus analogous to ArbTEDS. Using XNLI is therefore both a dataset change and a necessity: there is no drop-in Hindi ArbTEDS. Code-mixing (Hindi--English) appears in real user text and only sparsely in XNLI translations; we do not claim robustness to social-media Hinglish.

Taken together, switching Arabic to Hindi is not a cosmetic find-and-replace. It forces a different script, a different morphology, and a different public dataset, which is why options 1 and 6 of the assignment are satisfied simultaneously.

\section{The Original Lexical Method}
\label{sec:khader}

Reconstructing \citet{khader2016textual} makes the contrast with fine-tuning precise.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
Pairs are reversed from Buckwalter transliteration into Arabic script, then tagged and stemmed. Named entities are marked in the released dataset. POS tags combine MSTParser and MADA (the authors cite 81\% tagging accuracy on the Penn Arabic Treebank). Stems come from the ISRI stemmer. Stop words are then removed, with the acknowledged risk that negation particles are lost~\parencite{alkhawaldeh2015negation}.

\subsection{Unigram overlap and synonyms}
Let $T$ and $H$ be bags of stems after stop-word removal. Word overlap is the size of the intersection, with synonyms of a token in $T$ counting as a match to $H$ (the paper's example is \textit{ibn} / \textit{najl}, both ``son''). Entailment is predicted only if the overlap exceeds a threshold $\tau_u$ chosen by experiment. This is a linear decision in a one-dimensional feature:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{overlap}(T,H)
  = \bigl|\{\,w \in H : w \in T \;\vee\; \mathrm{syn}(w)\cap T \neq \emptyset\,\}\bigr|.
\end{equation}
No learning is applied to $\tau_u$ beyond grid search on the 100-pair sample.

\subsection{Bigram match}
Following \citet{du2010recognize}, they compute
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BigramMatch}(T,H)
  = \frac{\#\{\text{bigrams of }H\text{ that occur in }T\}}{\#\{\text{bigrams of }H\}}.
\end{equation}
A second threshold $\tau_b$ is applied. The final label is Entails if both tests pass, otherwise NotEntails. The architecture (their Figure~1) is strictly feed-forward: preprocess, lexical/semantic matching, binary decision. There is no training set in the neural sense; the 100 pairs are both the development sample for thresholds and the reported test set.

\subsection{Why this fails to port to Hindi}
A Hindi replica would need a Hindi stemmer, a Hindi synonym lexicon, Hindi stop lists, and a new pair of thresholds. Even then, three-way XNLI labels have no equivalent of $\tau_u$: ``neutral'' is not ``overlap in between.'' Neural fine-tuning sidesteps that feature engineering. The encoder sees subword IDs, including negation particles, and the classification head is trained with cross-entropy on 392k labeled pairs.

The authors close by suggesting POS-weighted overlap and extra credit for named entities~\parencite{du2010recognize} as future work. They do not propose a neural model. Our change of algorithm (option~3) is therefore a break from their stated roadmap, not an implementation of a paragraph they already wrote.

Direct numeric comparison is therefore only a rubric check (``did we beat 61\% on our task?''), not a claim that XLM-R would score 75\% on ArbTEDS or that overlap would score 61\% on XNLI-hi.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Encoder}
Let $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ be the tokenized pair with $n\le 128$. XLM-RoBERTa~\parencite{conneau2020xlmr} is a 12-block Transformer encoder~\parencite{vaswani2017attention}. In each block, queries, keys, and values $Q,K,V$ for a head are linear maps of the residual stream, and
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)
  = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right)V
\end{equation}
with $d_h=64$ (12 heads $\times$ 64 $=$ 768). A position-wise MLP then expands to 3{,}072 units and back. Residual connections and layer normalization follow the RoBERTa~\parencite{liu2019roberta} layout. There is no token-type embedding (XLM-R's type vocabulary size is 1); two consecutive separator tokens mark the premise/hypothesis boundary. We load \texttt{xlm-roberta-base} and replace the masked-language-model head with a sequence-classification head. All 278M parameters are trainable; we do not freeze the encoder. Dropout 0.1 remains at the values shipped in the pretrained config.

\subsection{Classification head}
Let $h_{\langle s\rangle} \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$ be the final-layer hidden state at the first token. The RoBERTa classification head is
\begin{align}
  z &= \tanh(W_d h_{\langle s\rangle} + b_d), \\
  \ell &= W_o z + b_o \in \mathbb{R}^{3},
\end{align}
with $W_d \in \mathbb{R}^{768\times 768}$ and $W_o \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 768}$ randomly initialized (the LOAD REPORT's ``MISSING'' rows). Dropout is applied before both linear maps according to the model config. Predicted class is $\arg\max_k \ell_k$. Training minimizes token-level cross-entropy
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}
  = -\log \frac{\exp(\ell_y)}{\sum_{k=0}^{2}\exp(\ell_k)},
\end{equation}
where $y\in\{0,1,2\}$ is the gold label. A uniform classifier has $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}]=\ln 3 \approx 1.099$, which is the loss we observed when training collapsed.

\subsection{Optimization}
We use AdamW~\parencite{loshchilov2019decoupled,kingma2015adam} with decoupled weight decay 0.01, learning rate $2\times 10^{-5}$, and a linear schedule with 10\% warmup. In Transformers 5.x the field \texttt{warmup\_steps} accepts a float in $[0,1)$ as a fraction of total steps; we set \texttt{warmup\_steps=0.1}. Batch size is 16, sequence length 128, seed 42, full precision (FP32). Checkpoints are written each epoch; the best validation accuracy is restored at the end. Table~\ref{tab:hp} summarizes the paper-run settings.

\subsection{Metrics}
We report accuracy and \emph{macro}-averaged precision, recall, and F1. Macro averaging treats the three classes equally, so a one-class predictor cannot hide behind 33\% accuracy. \citet{khader2016textual} reported per-label and overall precision/recall for a two-way task; we cite their 61\% overall P/R as the original baseline and do not convert it into a three-way F1.

\subsection{Apple Silicon training stack}
Transformers v5, when PyTorch $\geq 2.8$, defaults to \texttt{adamw\_torch\_fused}. Fused AdamW on MPS is a beta kernel. Combined with the library default of scaled dot-product attention (SDPA), a 5\%/1-epoch run produced:
\begin{itemize}
  \item training loss stuck at $1.09$--$1.11$ for 1{,}228 steps;
  \item healthy-looking gradient norms ($0.9$--$6.9$);
  \item validation accuracy exactly $1/3$, macro-precision $0.1111$, macro-recall $0.3333$, macro-F1 $0.1667$;
  \item $\langle s\rangle$ hidden states with cosine similarity $\approx 1$ across different pairs (representation collapse), even though encoder weights had moved only $\sim 10^{-3}$ from initialization.
\end{itemize}
The XNLI columns, tokenizer pairing, and \texttt{label}$\rightarrow$\texttt{labels} collator were intact (diverse \texttt{input\_ids}, mixed labels). The failure was the optimizer/attention path, not missing targets. The production code therefore sets \texttt{attn\_implementation="eager"} and \texttt{optim="adamw\_torch"}, and does not call \texttt{model.to("mps")} before the Trainer (the Trainer places the model). After that change, a 5\% run showed train loss falling through $1.05$ then below $1.0$ in the second half of the epoch---the signature of an actual learning signal.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Software}
Python 3.14, PyTorch 2.12--2.14, Transformers 5.17, Datasets, and Evaluate. Scripts: \texttt{src/train.py} (fine-tune), \texttt{src/eval\_nli.py} (test metrics and confusion matrix), \texttt{src/inference.py} (six Hindi demo pairs). \subsection{Protocol}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Load \texttt{facebook/xnli} / \texttt{hi}. Optionally take the first $p$ fraction of train (\texttt{--subset}).
  \item Tokenize premise--hypothesis pairs to length 128.
  \item Fine-tune as in Table~\ref{tab:hp}. Select the epoch with highest validation accuracy.
  \item Save tokenizer and weights to \texttt{output/model}.
  \item Run \texttt{src/eval\_nli.py} on the 5{,}010-pair test split; write \texttt{results/metrics.json} and \texttt{results/confusion\_matrix.png}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Runs}
\textbf{Sanity (5\%, 1 epoch).} 19{,}635 pairs, 1{,}227 steps/epoch, about 25 minutes at $\sim$13 samples/s. Purpose: confirm loss leaves $\ln 3$ and validation accuracy leaves 33.3\%. \TBD{Sanity val acc.\ / F1 from the current timing-run.}

  \item \citet{khader2016textual}: 61\% P/R, two-way, 100 Arabic pairs.

\paragraph{What would count as success.}
If test accuracy is $33.3\%$, the run failed (collapsed head). If it is in the $50$--$65\%$ band on a 5\%/1-epoch sanity check, the pipeline is learning and the full three-epoch run is worth the wall-clock. If the full run lands in the mid-70s, we have reproduced the expected XLM-R-base level~\parencite{conneau2020xlmr} and clearly beaten $61\%$ P/R on a harder label set. If it exceeds $80\%$, we should sanity-check for test leakage (there should be none: XNLI test is a fixed split). We will not claim $85\%$ unless the JSON file actually contains that number.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:disc}

\paragraph{Translated test set.}
XNLI-Hindi hypotheses are translations of English MultiNLI~\parencite{conneau2018xnli}. A model can exploit translationese (more explicit pronouns, more regular word order) that would not appear in naturally authored Hindi news. ArbTEDS, by contrast, is naturally Arabic. That is another reason not to over-interpret a 15-point gap versus \citet{khader2016textual}.

\paragraph{Length 128.}
Some XNLI premises are multi-sentence. Truncation can delete the clause that licenses or blocks $H$. A 256-token run is a cheap ablation we did not schedule on M5.

\paragraph{Base vs.\ large.}
XLM-R-large would likely raise accuracy several points~\parencite{conneau2020xlmr} at roughly $4\times$ memory and time. For a course deadline, base is the right default.

\paragraph{No matched Arabic rerun.}
We did not reimplement overlap+bigram on Hindi XNLI, nor XLM-R on ArbTEDS. Either experiment would tighten the comparison; both are future work.

\paragraph{Hardware specificity.}
The fused-AdamW/SDPA collapse is an MPS observation. CUDA users can keep fused kernels. The scientific result (fine-tuning XLM-R on XNLI-hi) does not depend on Apple hardware; the engineering note does.

\paragraph{Ethics.}
XNLI contains MultiNLI genres (including the 9/11 report). We use the corpus only as a public benchmark. The demo pairs in \texttt{inference.py} are synthetic and contain no personal data.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}

\citet{khader2016textual} showed that lexical overlap plus synonyms can reach 61\% P/R on a small Arabic RTE sample. We asked the same question for Hindi and answered it with a multilingual Transformer. Changing language, dataset, algorithm, and training method---and shipping runnable code---satisfies the course requirement of at least two adaptations, and in practice five of the six listed options. The remaining option (improved results) is an empirical claim: \TBD{test accuracy XX.X\%, macro-F1 YY.Y\%, against 61\% P/R in the original system and the mid-70s XLM-R-base XNLI-Hindi range in prior work~\parencite{conneau2020xlmr}.}

Future work is straightforward: XLM-R-large, length 256, a Hindi-original test set, and a true overlap baseline on the same pairs so the lexical vs.\ neural gap is measured in one language.
